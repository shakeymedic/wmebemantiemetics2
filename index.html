Choosing the Right Antiemetic in the ED

A Quick Guide for Clinicians

Syndrome-based framework adapted from Glare et al., Clinical Interventions in Aging (2011). Doses and safety warnings checked against MHRA, product SmPCs, RCOG and RCEM guidance (October 2026) – see References.

1. Assess the Patient: What is the Nausea's Story?

A quick history can point to the underlying cause and the most effective treatment pathway.

Gastric Stasis / Impaired Emptying

  • Patient says: "I feel full really quickly," "I'm bloated," "Vomiting helps a bit."
  • Vomit is: Small volume, may contain undigested food.
  • Think: Gastroparesis, opioids, ascites.

PATHWAY: GI Tract (Dopamine, Serotonin)

Chemical / Toxin-Mediated

  • Patient says: "The nausea is constant," "Smells make it worse," "Vomiting doesn't help."
  • Think: Metabolic (uraemia, hypercalcaemia), medications, toxins.

PATHWAY: Chemoreceptor Trigger Zone (CTZ)

Vestibular

  • Patient says: "I feel dizzy," "It's worse when I move my head."
  • Think: Motion sickness, labyrinthitis, CNS metastases.

PATHWAY: Vestibular Nucleus

Bowel Obstruction

  • Patient says: "I have crampy tummy pain," "I haven't opened my bowels."
  • Vomit is: Large volume, may become bilious or faeculent.
  • Think: Mechanical obstruction, ileus, constipation.

PATHWAY: GI Tract & Visceral Afferents

Cortical / Anxiety-Driven

  • Patient says: "I feel very anxious," "The thought of it makes me sick."
  • Think: Anxiety, anticipatory nausea.
  • Not this pathway: early-morning nausea/vomiting with headache suggests raised ICP – assess and image for the cause; do not treat as anxiety.

PATHWAY: Cerebral Cortex (GABA)

2. Match the Cause to the Drug Class

Clinical Syndrome First-Line Drug Class Drug Examples (UK) Mechanism
Gastric Stasis Prokinetic (D₂ Antagonist) Metoclopramide Stimulates gut motility. Short-term use only (max 5 days, MHRA).
Chemical/Toxin Dopamine D₂ Antagonist Haloperidol, Prochlorperazine Blocks the CTZ from detecting emetogenic substances.
Vestibular Antihistamine (H₁) / Anticholinergic Cyclizine, Prochlorperazine Blocks signals from the inner ear to the vomiting centre.
Bowel Obstruction Antisecretory / Antimuscarinic Cyclizine, Hyoscine Butylbromide Reduces secretions and spasm. AVOID prokinetics. Hyoscine butylbromide injection: caution in cardiac disease, contraindicated in tachycardia (MHRA 2017).
Cortical/Anxiety Benzodiazepine Lorazepam (as an adjunct) Anxiolytic and amnesic properties.

3. Quick Reference: Antiemetic Toolkit

Drug Class Primary Use Case Pearls for the ED
Metoclopramide D₂ Antagonist (Prokinetic) Gastric Stasis. Mild chemical nausea. Adult dose: 60 kg and over: 10 mg up to 3 times daily (max 30 mg in 24 h). Under 60 kg: max 500 micrograms/kg in 24 h, in 3 divided doses. Max 5 days (MHRA 2013; BNF). IV as a slow bolus over at least 3 minutes. Risk of EPSEs (dystonia, akathisia), esp. in children and young adults, even after one dose. Contraindicated in mechanical GI obstruction, GI haemorrhage or perforation, phaeochromocytoma, Parkinson's disease and with levodopa/dopamine agonists. Not for children under 1 year.
Ondansetron 5HT₃ Antagonist Visceral Nausea (gastroenteritis: off-label in adults; no BNF adult dose for this use). Post-op. Chemo-induced. Can cause constipation & headache. Not for motion sickness. QT: avoid in congenital long QT; caution with other QT-prolonging drugs, heart failure or bradyarrhythmia; correct low K+/Mg2+ first (MHRA 2012/2013). Licensed PONV IV dose is a single 4 mg. Pregnancy: small increased risk of oral clefts with first-trimester use (MHRA 2020) – second line, after counselling.
Cyclizine H₁ Antihistamine Vestibular Nausea. Bowel obstruction. Adult dose 50 mg up to 3 times daily (oral, IM or slow IV). Can cause drowsiness & dry mouth. Caution in elderly (anticholinergic effects). Caution in severe heart failure or acute MI – may reduce cardiac output. Recognised potential for misuse, especially IV.
Haloperidol D₂ Antagonist (Butyrophenone) Chemical/Toxin-Mediated Nausea. "Broad-spectrum" option. Less sedating. Monitor for EPSEs. In the UK licensed for postoperative N&V only, in patients at moderate to high risk when other antiemetics have failed or are not tolerated (BNF): 1–2 mg IM, older adults 500 micrograms; other antiemetic use is off-label, and IV use needs continuous ECG monitoring. Baseline ECG and correct electrolytes first. Contraindicated with known QTc prolongation, other QT-prolonging drugs, Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies. RCEM suggests it for refractory cannabinoid hyperemesis.
Prochlorperazine D₂ Antagonist (Phenothiazine) Vestibular Nausea (labyrinthitis). Mild chemical nausea. Can be given buccally. Risk of dystonic reactions. Can cause sedation & hypotension.
Levomepromazine Broad Spectrum Refractory Nausea. Highly effective but very sedating. Typically reserved for palliative care.

4. Special Considerations: The Elderly Patient

  • "Start low, go slow." Older adults are more susceptible to side effects.
  • Beware of Anticholinergic Burden: Drugs like cyclizine can worsen confusion, glaucoma, and prostatic hypertrophy.
  • Metoclopramide: Increased risk of tardive dyskinesia and other extrapyramidal symptoms. Use with caution and consider a lower dose in frailty. Reduce the dose by 50% if CrCl 15–60 ml/min or severe hepatic impairment, and by 75% if CrCl ≤15 ml/min.
  • Benzodiazepines: Increased risk of falls and delirium. Use lorazepam if necessary (shorter action, inactive metabolites).

5. MHRA Safety Updates, Pregnancy & ED Evidence

  • Metoclopramide (MHRA, Aug 2013): short-term use only (up to 5 days); adults 60 kg and over 10 mg up to three times daily (max 30 mg in 24 h); adults under 60 kg max 500 micrograms/kg in 24 h; slow IV bolus over at least 3 minutes. In ages 1–18 second line only; contraindicated under 1 year.
  • Domperidone (MHRA, May 2014; Dec 2019): for nausea and vomiting only; max 10 mg three times daily (30 mg/24 h) orally, usually for no more than 1 week. Contraindicated in cardiac conduction disorders, underlying cardiac disease such as heart failure, with QT-prolonging drugs or potent CYP3A4 inhibitors, and in severe hepatic impairment. No longer licensed under 12 years or under 35 kg.
  • Ondansetron IV (MHRA, 2012–2013): dose-dependent QT prolongation. Avoid in congenital long QT; correct hypokalaemia/hypomagnesaemia first. Single IV dose for chemotherapy-induced N&V max 16 mg (8 mg if 75 years or older), infused over at least 15 minutes; PONV dose unchanged at 4 mg.
  • Hyoscine butylbromide injection (MHRA, Feb 2017): can cause tachycardia, hypotension and anaphylaxis, with fatal outcomes reported in patients with cardiac disease. Use with caution in cardiac disease, with resuscitation facilities to hand; contraindicated in tachycardia.
  • Pregnancy (RCOG Green-top 69, 2024): first line are H1 antihistamines, phenothiazines and doxylamine/pyridoxine (Xonvea). Ondansetron is second line and should not be discouraged if first-line drugs fail; counsel about the very small absolute increase in orofacial clefts with first-trimester use (MHRA 2020). Metoclopramide is safe and effective but second line because of EPSEs. Give thiamine to women admitted with vomiting. Use PUQE to grade severity; ketonuria does not indicate dehydration.
  • Cannabinoid hyperemesis (RCEM, Feb 2024): often resistant to standard antiemetics. For refractory symptoms consider haloperidol IM 0.05 mg/kg (max 5 mg) after an ECG, or topical capsaicin. Do not give haloperidol if the QTc is prolonged or the patient has Parkinson’s disease or Lewy body dementia.
  • ED evidence: the Cochrane review (Furyk 2015) found no definite evidence that any antiemetic is better than another, or than placebo, in adults in the ED; many patients improve with supportive care alone, so choose by cause, adverse-effect profile and patient preference. A 2025 network meta-analysis (deSouza et al.) ranked IV prochlorperazine among the most effective agents, oral ondansetron among the least effective, and IV metoclopramide, droperidol and promethazine among the most likely to cause adverse reactions.
